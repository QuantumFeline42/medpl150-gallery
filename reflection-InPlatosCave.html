A Reflection on 'In Plato's Cave' by Susan Sontag

I had never thought about photography as an aggressive act before, especially not in such stark terms and examples as Susan Sontag used. While it is sometimes treated as a joke directed at less advanced cultures that a camera would ‘steal someone’s soul,’ On Photography laid out many ways in which modern photography, for all the good it accomplishes, also does sap some of the soul out of how we live and how we view the world.

I’ve never been someone that did a lot of over-documenting of life’s events. More likely it’s been the opposite, and I’ve felt occasional regret at not taking enough photos of something that happened, whether a trip or party or entertainment event. Perhaps it comes from not growing up with a camera in my pocket, except for when I intentionally brought one. As a child of the 80s and 90s, taking photos usually meant disposable cameras (cheap, but still a conscious expense), and only so many shots available at a time. I never developed the habit of taking dozens of pictures just to get the best shot, or reaching for the camera as a reflex when something was happening.

Scrolling back through my camera feed these days the gaps of weeks or even months when there are few, if any, photos are more notable to me than the times when I remembered to record something special. Whereas before I would kick myself for not being more on top of personal documentation, having read On Photography I can perhaps be less harsh on myself. During those times I was enjoying the event and making my own memories, and the times that matter will stick with me even without photographic proof that it happened.

When it comes to image making there’s definitely more of a dedicated place for that in my life and mind. I’ve been drawing since I was a kid and, out of many in both my immediate and extended family, I was noted as one of the more artistic ones. I filled numerous sketch books with drawings and had childhood dreams of drawing comics or working in animation. While I didn’t end up landing in either of those career tracks I did get my Associate’s Degree in Graphic Design in my 20s to boost what became my career trajectory up until now.

Imagination is something that I delve into a lot in both my work and my hobbies, and creating images is something I tend to do to get something out of my head and into reality as best I can. As I shift that career and skill trajectory in my 40s towards Psychology, I don’t intend to leave image creation behind altogether. As someone who is neurodivergent and has often found that being able to visualize thoughts beyond just words has helped me to understand myself and others better, I hope that I can use the skills I’ve developed over the years and my natural inclinations toward visual thinking as a way to bridge the large gulf between consciousnesses to help people connect better and understand each other more.
