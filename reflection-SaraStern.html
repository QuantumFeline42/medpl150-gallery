A Reflection on the Works of Sara Stern

I’ve always been a huge fan of puppetry since I was a kid watching the Muppets so it was nice to see puppetry and stop-motion animation used by Sara as a method of artistic expression as it’s much more commonly used as a method of entertainment rather than art itself. Notably I was impressed by the puppets she created that were far removed from human analogues like the marble eyes in a wall, heads that look like they’re formed out of sand, and an entire building facade. We’re wired to see faces even when there are none and to see motions as human-like so it’s fascinating to see an artist take advantage of those evolved pattern-recognizing instincts to an extreme to make clearly non-living objects in the world seem alive.

Another feature of her recorded art pieces that stood out to me was the use of natural backdrops for both puppetry and stop-motion art pieces. At a professional level those forms of performance usually rely on tools and sets built to facilitate them, like the raised sets of Muppet productions or the scaled sets and controlled lighting of Laika studios stop-motion films. While the limited budget of a singular artist might be one reason for taking advantage of natural backdrops despite the additional difficulties it would introduce, the pieces Sara produced managed to not just work around the limitations but to incorporate them into the work, like how the sped-up passage of time (often the bane of live shoots outdoors) allowed for a sunset to be part of a relatively short film. It also brought other natural elements like waves, birds, fish, and (in the case of the talking building) a city itself into the scene, grounding the puppets and making them feel more like part of the world instead of fabricated creations.
